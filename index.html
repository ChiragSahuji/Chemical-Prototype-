Herbal mosquito repellent

A chemistry project, extracted, refined and made by hand in our lab.

Why oil and water need a go-between

Aim. To prepare a herbal mosquito repellent spray from citronella or lemongrass essential oil.

Principle. Essential oil doesn't dissolve in water, so ethanol is used as the solvent that carries the oil into the water. Glycerin acts as a humectant and fixative, helping the scent last a little longer on skin. Distilled water makes up the bulk of the volume.

Try it on the right. Shake plain oil and water and they split again within seconds. Dissolve the oil in ethanol first, add water, and the oil stays dispersed as a fine haze.

Oil phase · 2 mL

Why we used castor oil

It was the only oil available in our lab, so it took the oil-phase role in our batch. It turned out to be a sensible stand-in for demonstrating the process:

  • It dissolves in ethanol. Most fatty oils don't. Castor oil's main fatty acid, ricinoleic acid, carries a hydroxyl (–OH) group, which lets it mix with alcohol.
  • It's thick and slow to evaporate, so the film it leaves on skin lasts longer than a thin oil.
  • It's gentle on skin and has little smell, so the scent is easy to judge.

Honest limit: castor oil is not a recognised mosquito repellent. Our prototype shows the formulation method. For real protection, replace it with citronella or lemongrass oil (or add one alongside it).

Reference active · 2 mL

Citronella or lemongrass oil

The oil the original formula calls for. Citronella (Cymbopogon nardus or winterianus) is rich in citronellal and geraniol; lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus) in citral. These scents mask the cues mosquitoes use to find skin, but they evaporate quickly, which is why a fixative is added.

Why it's in the formula

It's the active repellent. Everything else in the bottle exists to carry it, spread it and keep it on the skin.

Solvent · 20 mL (70–95%)

Ethanol

A molecule with two personalities: a polar –OH end that mixes with water and a non-polar ethyl end that mixes with oil. That's what lets it bridge the two. It also evaporates fast, so the spray dries on skin.

Why not methanol?

Methanol is toxic and must not go into anything sprayed on skin. Use ethanol only. Ethanol is also flammable, so keep it away from flames.

Humectant and fixative · 2–3 mL

Glycerin

Glycerol has three –OH groups, so it holds on to water and to skin moisture. It's thick and barely evaporates, which slows the loss of the scented oil and leaves the skin feeling less dry than ethanol alone.

Why only a little?

Too much makes the spray sticky and slow to dry. 2 to 3 mL in 50 mL is enough to act as a fixative.

Diluent · 25 mL

Distilled water

Makes up half the volume and keeps ethanol's sting down. It is distilled because dissolved minerals in tap water can cloud the mixture and give microbes something to feed on.

Why add it slowly?

Water is the one ingredient the oil doesn't like. Added slowly while stirring, the oil breaks into tiny droplets instead of clumping.

    Experimental setup and safety

    What was on the bench, and what we did to keep a flammable solvent and a stirring motor from becoming a problem.

    Tap a pin to see how the stirring station is set up.

    Our setup

    Mixing vessel
    A glass beaker, large enough that the 50 mL batch stays below the halfway mark and can swirl without splashing.
    Stirring
    Glass rod for the gentle first mixes. A REMI 2MLH magnetic stirrer at about 640 rpm for the long stirring stages.
    Measuring
    Measuring cylinder for ethanol and water, dropper for the oil, read at eye level at the bottom of the meniscus.
    Transfer
    A funnel (with filter paper if the mixture is turbid) into a clean spray bottle.
    Ventilation
    The lab's exhaust fans stay running. Ethanol vapour is heavier than air and collects at bench level.
